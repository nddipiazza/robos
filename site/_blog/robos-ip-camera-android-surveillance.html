A spare phone already has a good lens, a battery backup, and Wi-Fi. After putting LineageOS 24 (Android 17) and RobOS on an old Moto G100 and building RobOS Camera Watch on the desktop, the natural next step was turning a RobOS phone into a real network surveillance camera: plug it into wall power, point it at a room or entryway, add it to Camera Watch and Smart Home, and leave it running continuously with no cloud account and no vendor relay.

Why ordinary Android camera apps stop when you walk away. Treating an Android 14–17 phone like dedicated IP camera firmware means solving four OS behaviors that normally shut down camera apps within minutes:

  1. Activity preview surfaces vanish when the screen turns off. Most camera apps bind their Camera2 CameraCaptureSession to a Compose/View SurfaceView or TextureView. Locking the phone, switching apps, or pressing the physical Power button destroys that surface and halts capture.
  2. Wi-Fi power-save spikes latency and drops multicast. With the screen dark, Android puts the Wi-Fi radio into PS-Poll power save (our Moto G100's ping jumped to 297 ms) and filters out UDP multicast packets—silently breaking ONVIF discovery on 239.255.255.250:3702.
  3. Doze and Android 14+ foreground service rules. Starting or holding a camera session in the background requires an explicit foregroundServiceType="camera" declaration, runtime camera permission checks before startForeground(), and battery-optimization exemption.
  4. Per-frame Bitmap allocations overheat long-running streams. Allocating fresh Bitmap objects at 10–15 fps triggers constant garbage collection and warms up the phone over multi-day runs.

Decoupling Camera2 from the screen. In Camera2Engine.kt, the CameraCaptureSession targets a single off-screen ImageReader.newInstance(w, h, ImageFormat.YUV_420_888, 3) on a dedicated HandlerThread("robos-camera2"). When MainActivity is visible on screen, it receives a throttled ~4 fps preview bitmap; when you leave the app or turn the display off with the Power button, UI bitmap decoding drops to zero while the network stream continues uninterrupted. For wall mounts where you want a faint status clock at night, tapping Dim Screen switches to an OLED Blackout overlay (screenBrightness = 0.01f on pure #000000) that drifts slowly every 15 seconds to avoid burn-in.

Zero-GC rotation, 5×7 pixel OSD, and luma motion detection. FrameProcessor.kt copies the three Camera2 planes into pre-allocated byte arrays, rotates 0° / 90° / 180° / 270° directly in YUV space, burns a crisp 5×7 bitmap timestamp bar (XT2125-4 CAMERA | 2026-11-19 15:18:47) straight into the NV21 bytes without touching android.graphics.Canvas, and downsamples a 96 × 54 grid from the Y luminance plane for on-camera motion detection matching packages/camera-watch/lib/motion-detector.js.

Two real bugs caught during live verification. First, our initial snapshot from the Moto G100 came back upside down when the phone lay in reverse landscape (Surface.ROTATION_270): Android's OrientationEventListener reports degrees clockwise while Surface.ROTATION_* is counter-clockwise, so back-facing sensors need (sensorOrientation + orientationDegrees) % 360 seeded from Display.rotation at startup before gravity events fire. Second, our JUnit suite (IpCameraCoreTest.kt) caught a subtle WS-Security bug before deployment: a naive <Username[^>]*> regex matched the enclosing <UsernameToken> tag instead of <Username> when validating OASIS PasswordDigest (Base64(SHA1(Nonce + Created + Password))).

On the Moto G100: IP Camera, Smart Home Cameras tab, and RobOS Home

Solid-state PTZ without motors. Because the Moto G100's Camera2 HAL exposes SENSOR_INFO_ACTIVE_ARRAY_SIZE and SCALER_CROP_REGION, HttpOnvifServer.kt maps ONVIF ContinuousMove (pan, tilt, zoom) and GotoPreset directly onto silent sensor crop windows across the high-resolution sensor, plus hardware presets for 1x Wide (Back), 2x Zoom, 4x Zoom, Front Camera, and Toggle Flashlight.

Verify it yourself: screen-off streaming, ONVIF discovery & tests

With the phone unlocked over USB on the same Wi-Fi network, we built and installed the APKs, discovered the phone from desktop Camera Watch via WS-Discovery, and powered the phone's screen completely off with KEYCODE_SLEEP (mAwake=false) to confirm frames kept flowing at 10.0 fps:

# 1. Run JVM unit tests for RobOS IP Camera, Smart Home Android, and desktop Camera Watch (cd packages/ip-camera-android && ./gradlew testDebugUnitTest) (cd packages/smart-home-android && ./gradlew testDebugUnitTest) node --test packages/robos-test/tests/camera-watch/unit.test.js # 2. Build & install onto any Android 11+ phone with USB debugging enabled (cd packages/ip-camera-android && ./gradlew assembleDebug && adb install -r app/build/outputs/apk/debug/app-debug.apk) (cd packages/smart-home-android && ./gradlew assembleDebug && adb install -r app/build/outputs/apk/debug/app-debug.apk) # 3. Verify ONVIF WS-Discovery + live telemetry + screen-off persistence over Wi-Fi curl -s http://192.168.1.65:8080/api/status | python3 -m json.tool adb shell input keyevent KEYCODE_SLEEP # mAwake=false, mScreenOnFully=false sleep 5 # framesServed: 1087 -> 1151 (+64 frames with display off) curl -s -o snapshot.jpg http://192.168.1.65:8080/snapshot.jpg

Limits to keep in mind. Running a camera sensor and Wi-Fi radio 24/7 needs continuous wall power; the Live tab warns whenever the phone is discharging on battery. On Android 15+, background restrictions may prevent BOOT_COMPLETED from starting a camera foreground service directly on stock ROMs without opening the activity or tapping the boot-resume notification. And streams on your LAN use HTTP/MJPEG by default for universal ffmpeg and browser compatibility—turn on Keystore-backed password protection on shared networks, and use Camera Watch's mutual-TLS 1.3 invites when sharing views between computers.
